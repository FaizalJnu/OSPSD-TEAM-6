Level 1: Implement One Operation
Each team member must take primary responsibility for implementing one distinct, useful public operation through all five specification levels, including its tests and documentation, with all operations integrated into the team’s shared service.
1.1 Your goal
Implement one public operation for your service.
This should be the smallest operation that represents the kind of service your team is building.
For example, your team might choose to retrieve one resource:
GET /<resource-type>/{id}
A resource could be an event, message, channel, file, folder, issue, task, or another domain object appropriate for your vertical.
The exact operation is up to your team.
For this level, the operation does not need to contact the real provider yet.
1.2. What should happen?
A caller should be able to make one request to your service and receive a well-defined response.
For example:
GET /<resource-type>/resource-123
might return:
{
  "id": "resource-123",
  "name": "Example resource"
}
Your actual route, resource type, and response fields should reflect your vertical.
The example above illustrates the shape of the requirement. It is not a required API.
For now, your implementation may return fixed or locally defined data.
1.3. Specification
Define one public operation and document its contract.
At minimum, specify:
the HTTP method;
the route;
any required path, query, or body parameters;
the successful response;
the successful status code;
what the operation means.
Your implementation must behave consistently with that contract.
The request and response should describe your service's domain rather than copying the structure of a future provider SDK.
Your API documentation, implementation, examples, and tests should agree with one another.
1.4. What you need to think about
Before implementing, agree as a team on:
What resource or action does this operation represent?
What should the caller provide?
What is the smallest useful response?
Which fields are part of your public contract?
Which fields are unnecessary?
What should callers be allowed to rely on?
Does the operation only read state, or does it change state?
What assumptions are you making that are not yet specified?
Write down any assumption that affects observable behavior.
Do not design the rest of your API yet.
1.5. Design and testing
Write at least one test for the successful behavior of the operation.
The test should exercise the public behavior of your service rather than internal implementation details.
For example, if your contract says that:
GET /<resource-type>/{id}
returns a resource containing an id and a name, your test should verify that behavior.
Avoid tests that depend on specific classes, helper functions, or internal data structures unless those details are themselves part of the public contract.
1.6. Team integration
Agree on the public contract before splitting the implementation work.
At least one teammate other than the author should review:
the route;
the request shape;
the response shape;
naming;
tests;
documentation.
The operation you define here will become the starting point for later levels.
Keep it small enough that your team can still change it easily.
1.7. You don't need these yet
You are not required to:
contact the real external provider;
authenticate with a provider;
define the full provider-independent interface;
use dependency injection;
handle every possible failure;
support collections;
implement pagination;
support retries or idempotency;
build your final workflow.
Do not add complexity simply because you expect to need it later.
1.8. When you're done
This level is complete when:
your service exposes one documented public operation;
a caller can invoke it and receive the expected response;
the behavior is covered by at least one test;
your documentation matches the implementation;
your team can explain what the operation guarantees to its caller.
The operation may still use fixed or locally defined data.
In the next level, you will make the same operation talk to a real provider.
